Computer audio

Alto plays notes: when you answer correctly, when you press “hear it” on an instrument, when an arpeggio is sounded. That needs very little code, but a few ideas about how computers represent and play sound, and a few about how instruments sound, are what make the code readable and what let you change it safely. This chapter starts with those ideas, then walks through alto/audio.py.

Sound as numbers

Sound is air pressure varying over time. A microphone turns it into a voltage, and an analogue-to-digital converter measures that voltage many times a second. The list of measurements is a digital audio signal. Playing it back reverses the process.

Three numbers describe a recording:

  • Sample rate: how many measurements per second. Alto uses 44,100 Hz (the Compact Disc rate; SAMPLE_RATE in audio.py).
  • Bit depth: how precisely each measurement is stored. WAV files in Alto are 16-bit: each sample is an integer from -32,768 to 32,767. Each bit adds about 6 dB of dynamic range (the gap between the quietest and loudest sound), so 16 bits give about 96 dB.
  • Channels: one (mono) or two (stereo). Alto’s recordings and everything it makes are mono.

The encoding of plain samples like this is called PCM, pulse-code modulation. A WAV file is a PCM stream with a small header (the RIFF container) saying the rate, depth and channels. Python’s standard wave module reads and writes them, which is all audio.py needs:

with wave.open(str(path)) as w:
    samples = np.frombuffer(w.readframes(w.getnframes()), dtype="<i2").astype(np.float64) / 32768

"<i2" means “little-endian signed 16-bit integer”; dividing by 32,768 turns the integers into floating-point numbers between -1 and 1, which are easier to compute with. Writing does the reverse: multiply by 32,767 and store as "<i2".

The sampling theorem and aliasing

The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem says that a signal can be captured perfectly if it is sampled at more than twice its highest frequency. At 44,100 Hz, nothing above 22,050 Hz can be represented, which is just above what people hear. A frequency that is too high is not lost but folded back down to a wrong, lower one. That is aliasing, and it is why recording equipment filters before sampling. It matters in Alto only when you change a sample’s speed (see below) or generate a sound with many high partials, which is why synth_piano stops adding partials above 12,000 Hz.

Loudness

  • Clipping: a sample cannot go beyond -1 or 1. A calculation that goes past it is flattened (“clipped”), which sounds harsh. Anything you generate should be scaled down (normalized) so its peak is below 1. synth_piano scales its result so the loudest sample is 0.8.
  • Decibels are a logarithmic scale for amplitude: 20 * log10(ratio). Halving the amplitude is -6 dB. Because hearing is roughly logarithmic too, a linear volume of 0.5 does not sound “half as loud”; it sounds only a little quieter. Qt’s QSoundEffect.setVolume is linear. If you want a volume control that feels even, map it through a curve (Qt provides QtAudio.convertVolume for that).
  • Summing sounds adds amplitudes, so a chord of four notes is louder, and can clip. Alto’s play_notes plays each note of a chord at 1.4 / sqrt(count) of full volume: that keeps the loudness of a chord about the same however many notes it has, because uncorrelated signals add in power (the square of amplitude), not in amplitude.

Pitch, frequency and the numbers behind notes

A musical pitch is a frequency in hertz (cycles per second). Western music divides the octave into 12 equal ratios, equal temperament, so each semitone multiplies the frequency by 2^(1/12). MIDI (the Musical Instrument Digital Interface standard) numbers the notes: middle C is 60, A above it is 69, and each semitone adds one. The reference pitch A4 is 440 Hz (set by an international standard, ISO 16). The formula in synth_piano is the whole story:

f0 = 440.0 * 2 ** ((midi - 69) / 12)  # midi 60 gives 261.63 Hz

Note.midi in alto/theory/pitch.py computes the MIDI number from a spelled note. Note that C♯4 and D♭4 have the same MIDI number and the same frequency (in equal temperament), which is why audio works with MIDI numbers while the rest of Alto works with spelled notes.

A real note is not a pure tone. It is a fundamental at f0 plus overtones, or partials, at higher frequencies. In a string or a pipe the partials are close to whole-number multiples (the harmonic series: 2×, 3×, 4× …), and the mix of their strengths is what we hear as timbre, the difference between a flute and a viola playing the same note.

How Alto plays a note

Everything is in the class Sounds and a single shared instance, sounds, in alto/audio.py.

Instruments and recordings

An Instrument (a frozen dataclass) has a key, a label, a folder under alto/assets/ and a few flags: gain (its level relative to the piano), sustained and, for instruments with several ways of playing (the viola’s arco and pizzicato), a family and a variant. Recordings are one mono WAV file per note, named by MIDI number: alto/assets/samples/flute/72.wav is the flute’s C5. Most come from the University of Iowa Electronic Music Studios’ Musical Instrument Samples, recorded in a studio, in most cases at every semitone.

An instrument that plays one recording for each pitch is a sampler, and the technique is multisampling. Professional samplers add more: several recordings per note at different loudnesses (velocity layers), alternating takes to avoid a machine-gun repetition (round robin), and repeating a section of a long note to sustain it (looping). Alto needs none of these, since each note is played once at one level.

Creating the sound

play_midi(midi)
   -> _effect(inst, midi)          get or make a QSoundEffect for (instrument, note)
        -> _note_file(inst, midi)  find the WAV; make one if the note has no recording
   -> setVolume(...), play()
  1. _note_file returns the recording’s path if that note has one. If it does not, it picks the nearest recorded note and makes a new WAV by shifting its pitch (below), saved in alto_sounds in the system temporary folder. The piano is special: beyond two semitones from a recording it is synthesized instead (below).
  2. _effect wraps the file in a QSoundEffect the first time and keeps it, so a note is loaded once. QSoundEffect is Qt Multimedia’s class for short, uncompressed sounds that must start with low delay: it loads the whole file into memory, so play() is quick. Qt has two others: QMediaPlayer streams longer, compressed audio and starts slower, and QAudioSink is a lower-level output that you feed raw samples yourself. Short WAV notes are what QSoundEffect is for.
  3. play_midi sets the volume (the requested volume times the instrument’s gain, capped at 1) and plays. It is wrapped in try/except and prints rather than raises: audio must never break an exercise, and machines with no sound card exist. sounds.enabled is the master switch the sound button controls; force=True plays anyway, for the instrument preview.

Pitch shifting by resampling

def shift_pitch(samples, *, semitones):
    ratio = 2 ** (semitones / 12)
    positions = np.arange(0, len(samples) - 1, ratio)
    return np.interp(positions, np.arange(len(samples)), samples)

To raise a recording by two semitones, read it 12% faster: take the samples at positions 0, 1.122, 2.245, … and interpolate between neighbors. This is resampling, and it is the simplest pitch shifter. It has two well-known side effects: the sound gets shorter (a note shifted up two semitones lasts 1/1.122 of the time, which the arithmetic above confirms: 70,560 samples become 62,861), and the timbre changes, because everything speeds up together (the “chipmunk effect”). For one or two semitones on a flute or a viola neither is noticeable, which is why Alto shifts only to fill the odd missing note.

If you ever need large shifts, there are better algorithms, each a topic of its own:

  • Phase vocoder: works in the frequency domain, so it can change pitch without changing duration, at some cost in clarity.
  • PSOLA (pitch-synchronous overlap-add): cuts the sound into one-period grains and overlaps them at new spacing; good for speech and single voices.
  • Better interpolation: np.interp is linear interpolation, which smooths the high frequencies slightly. Sinc or polynomial interpolation is more accurate (a polyphase resampler such as scipy.signal.resample_poly).

Making a piano with arithmetic

synth_piano(midi) makes a piano note without any recording. It is additive synthesis: build the sound by adding sine waves, one per partial. The code has several pieces of real physics, each one line:

f0 = 440.0 * 2 ** ((midi - 69) / 12)
for n in range(1, 17):  # the first 16 partials
    f = n * f0 * np.sqrt(1 + inharm * n * n)  # stiff strings: partials sit slightly sharp
    amp = 1.0 / n**1.15  # higher partials are quieter
    if n % 7 == 0:
        amp *= 0.3  # the hammer strikes ~1/7 along the string, which suppresses these
    decay = 2.2 + 1.1 * n + (midi - 60) * 0.03  # higher partials, and higher notes, die faster
    for detune in (-0.0008, 0.0008):  # two strings per note, slightly apart: the shimmer
        out += amp * 0.5 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * (1 + detune) * t + phase) * np.exp(-decay * t)
  • Inharmonicity. A stiff string’s partials are not exact multiples of the fundamental; they are slightly sharper, by a factor of sqrt(1 + B n^2) for a constant B of the string. This is why a piano’s octaves are tuned wide, and why a pure harmonic sound would not sound like a piano.
  • Decay is an exponential, exp(-decay * t); higher partials fade quicker, so the sound mellows as it rings.
  • Strike point. Where the hammer hits a string, the partials that have a node there are not excited.
  • Envelope. After the partials come an attack of a couple of milliseconds (1 - exp(-t / 0.002)), a dip into the sustain, a short burst of filtered noise for the hammer’s thump (white noise smoothed by a moving average, a crude low-pass filter), and a 50 ms fade-out so the file does not end with a click.

You can check the result in a few lines. A Fourier transform shows the spectrum, and the loudest peak of a synthesized middle C is at 261.9 Hz (the frequency resolution of a 1.6 second clip is about 0.6 Hz, so this is the fundamental, 261.63 Hz):

import numpy as np
from alto.audio import synth_piano, SAMPLE_RATE

x = synth_piano(60)
spectrum = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x * np.hanning(len(x))))  # a window function reduces leakage
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x), 1 / SAMPLE_RATE)
print(freqs[spectrum.argmax()])

To listen, write it with to_wav_bytes and open the file:

from alto.audio import to_wav_bytes

open("/tmp/c4.wav", "wb").write(to_wav_bytes(synth_piano(60)))

Sustained and plucked sounds

A marimba bar, once struck, rings and fades whatever you do. A flute or a bowed string ends when the player stops. Instrument.sustained captures that: when a sustained instrument plays a note, any note still sounding is released by _release: a 70 ms fade of the effect’s volume property with a QPropertyAnimation, then stop(). Without that, melodies on a flute would pile up. stop_all cancels all fades and silences everything.

A piano is in between. Its recordings ring for a long time, but on a real piano a damper stops each string when the key is let go. A recording played in full sounds as if the damper pedal were held down: the notes of a scale pile up into a blur. Instrument.damped marks an instrument that is played without the pedal, and it gets the same release as a sustained one, so each note ends as the next begins, the way fingers move from key to key. The last note of a melody is left to fade by itself.

An abrupt stop is audible as a click, and a fade avoids it. The same idea (an envelope) shapes every synthesized note. The classical envelope is ADSR: attack (how fast the sound begins), decay, sustain level, release (how it ends).

Timing a sequence

To play a melody or an arpeggio, _play_sequence schedules each step with QTimer.singleShot and has three safeguards.

  1. Wait for loading. QSoundEffect loads in the background, and a note started before it is ready is silent. The sequence polls status() every 10 ms for up to a second before starting.
  2. Anchor the schedule to the start. Step i schedules step i + 1 a full gap after step i starts, not after it finishes. If starting one note takes a while, the others are not pushed later and the rhythm stays even. The test test_a_note_that_is_slow_to_start_does_not_upset_the_rhythm checks this.
  3. A newer sequence cancels an older one. Each sequence has a run number, and a step that finds a newer number does nothing. This pattern, a token, appears in the animations too: callbacks scheduled for later cannot be canceled easily, so they check whether they are still wanted.

Latency and the limits of this design

Latency is the delay between asking for a sound and hearing it. QSoundEffect is good enough here: a few tens of milliseconds. Real-time musical instruments need under about 10 ms, and for that you would use an audio library with a callback, such as PortAudio, and fill small buffers on a high-priority thread. If your program fills a buffer too late you get an underrun (a glitch). None of that applies to Alto, and it is the reason the playback code is short.

Adding an instrument

The steps are in For developers: the WAV files named by MIDI number in alto/assets/samples/<key>/, an Instrument in INSTRUMENTS, an entry in instrument_info.INFO, and the credit in THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md. Check that the license permits redistribution. A few practical points:

  • Trim silence at the start, or the note feels late. Normalize levels so that notes of one instrument are similar, then set gain to balance instruments against each other.
  • Mono, 16-bit files at any common rate (44.1 or 48 kHz) work; QSoundEffect resamples at playback.
  • Mark sustained=True for bowed and blown instruments, and damped=True for an instrument with dampers, such as the piano; leave both off for struck and plucked ones that ring on.
  • Gaps are fine: a missing note is made from its nearest neighbor.

Testing audio without speakers

Tests never play sound. tests/conftest.py turns sounds.enabled off for every test, and tests/test_audio.py replaces QSoundEffect with a FakeEffect that records play, stop and setVolume calls in a list, so assertions are about what would have been played, in what order and how loudly. This is the general approach to testing anything with a side effect on the world: replace the world with a recorder.

Words to search for

PCM, WAV, RIFF. Nyquist frequency, aliasing, anti-aliasing filter. Quantisation, dither. Dynamic range, decibel, loudness, psychoacoustics, equal-loudness contours. Overtone, partial, harmonic series, timbre, inharmonicity. Additive, subtractive, FM, wavetable and physical-modeling synthesis. Karplus-Strong algorithm (a famous few-line plucked-string model). Convolution and impulse response (how reverb is done). Fourier transform, FFT, spectrogram, window function. Resampling, interpolation, sinc interpolation, polyphase filter. Time-stretching, phase vocoder, PSOLA. Sampler, multisampling, velocity layer, round robin, loop point. SoundFont and SFZ (sample instrument formats). Latency, buffer, underrun, callback. MIDI (the standard also defines messages, channels and file formats).

Further reading

  • Julius O. Smith III, Mathematics of the Discrete Fourier Transform, Introduction to Digital Filters, Physical Audio Signal Processing and Spectral Audio Signal Processing (W3K Publishing, 2007; free at ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos, the author’s Stanford page, which is the primary source). The best free route from the sampling theorem to physical modeling of instruments.
  • Ken C. Pohlmann, Principles of Digital Audio, 6th ed. (McGraw-Hill, 2010): sampling, quantisation, converters, file formats.
  • Alan V. Oppenheim and Ronald W. Schafer, Discrete-Time Signal Processing, 3rd ed. (Pearson, 2010): the standard textbook on the mathematics behind everything above.
  • Claude E. Shannon, “Communication in the Presence of Noise”, Proceedings of the IRE 37(1), 1949: the sampling theorem.
  • Miller Puckette, The Theory and Technique of Electronic Music (World Scientific, 2007): a gentle book on building sound from oscillators and envelopes, and a good second read after this chapter.
  • Curtis Roads, The Computer Music Tutorial (MIT Press, 1996): the broad survey of the field.
  • Udo Zölzer (ed.), DAFX: Digital Audio Effects, 2nd ed. (Wiley, 2011): resampling, pitch shifting and time stretching, with the phase vocoder in detail.
  • Mark Dolson, “The Phase Vocoder: A Tutorial”, Computer Music Journal 10(4), 1986; and Jean Laroche and Mark Dolson, “Improved Phase Vocoder Time-Scale Modification of Audio”, IEEE Transactions on Speech and Audio Processing 7(3), 1999.
  • Kevin Karplus and Alex Strong, “Digital Synthesis of Plucked-String and Drum Timbres”, Computer Music Journal 7(2), 1983.
  • Harvey Fletcher, “Normal Vibration Frequencies of a Stiff Piano String”, Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 36(1), 1964: the inharmonicity formula used in synth_piano.
  • Neville H. Fletcher and Thomas D. Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments, 2nd ed. (Springer, 1998): why instruments sound as they do.
  • MIDI 1.0 Detailed Specification, MIDI Manufacturers Association, midi.org.
  • The University of Iowa Electronic Music Studios, Musical Instrument Samples, theremin.music.uiowa.edu/MIS.html: the source of the recordings Alto ships.
  • The Qt documentation for QSoundEffect, QMediaPlayer, QAudioSink and “Qt Multimedia”, doc.qt.io.
